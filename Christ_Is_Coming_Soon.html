Christ Is Coming Soon

Understanding the prophetic season we live in — and why being ready matters more than knowing the date.
Topic 1

Can We Know When?

The difference between knowing "the day and the hour" (impossible) and knowing "the generation" (possible)

When the disciples asked Jesus about the end of the age, He was very clear about one limit: no one can know the exact day or hour of His return.

Matthew 24:36

"But of that day and hour no one knows, not even the angels of heaven, but My Father only."

New King James Version (NKJV)

Yet that same passage draws a distinction between knowing the day and hour and knowing the generation or season. A little earlier, Jesus had said:

Matthew 24:34

"Assuredly, I say to you, this generation will by no means pass away till all these things take place."

New King James Version (NKJV)

This distinction is the foundation of this whole teaching: the goal is not to calculate a date on a calendar, but to recognize whether we are living within the "generation" or "season" that Scripture describes.

One discourse, two distinct historical periods. Why Matthew 24 answers three questions that do not happen at the same time.
Read more

Three questions, not one

In the Olivet Discourse (Matthew 24, Mark 13, Luke 21), the disciples ask Jesus three questions: (1) when will the temple be destroyed?, (2) what will be the sign of Your coming?, and (3) what will be the sign of the end of the age? These questions do not describe a single event — they span two historical periods separated by centuries.

Period one: 32-70 A.D.

The destruction of the temple was literally fulfilled in 70 A.D., when the Roman general Titus destroyed Jerusalem — forty years after Christ's death and resurrection.

Period two: 1948 onward

The signs of the second coming and of the end of the age, by contrast, connect with much more recent events: the rebirth of the nation of Israel in 1948, and the recapture of Jerusalem in 1967 — as explained in Topic 3. Recognizing that the discourse spans two separate periods avoids the confusion of trying to apply the entire passage to a single moment in history.

Topic 2

The Prophetic Precision of the First Coming

How one Jewish community anticipated, from Scripture alone, the exact timing of the first coming

Long before Jesus was born, the Essene community at Qumran — known for the Dead Sea Scrolls — studied the prophecies of Daniel and Zechariah in detail, and came to anticipate with striking precision the timing of the Messiah's entry into Jerusalem and His execution.

This reflects an important principle: the prophets themselves searched to understand the timing their own words pointed to.

1 Peter 1:10-11

"Of this salvation the prophets have inquired and searched carefully, who prophesied of the grace that would come to you, searching what, or what manner of time, the Spirit of Christ who was in them was indicating."

New King James Version (NKJV)

The same principle applies to the second coming: the day and hour cannot be predicted, but it is both reasonable and biblical to seek to understand the generation, or season.

The contrast with the Pharisees. Why building theology on isolated verses led to a misunderstanding about the Messiah.
Read more

Unlike the Essenes, the Pharisees built much of their messianic expectation on verses taken in isolation, such as:

Isaiah 9:6

"For unto us a Child is born, unto us a Son is given; and the government will be upon His shoulder..."

New King James Version (NKJV)

This passage speaks of both the Messiah's birth and the establishment of His government in the same breath — without indicating that a gap of time would separate the two. By failing to see the gap between the first coming (birth, suffering) and the full establishment of the kingdom, many in that era expected an immediate political and military Messiah, and did not recognize Jesus when He came in humility.

Topic 3

Israel: The Sign on God's Calendar

1948 and 1967 — two dates, two distinct biblical promises, both fulfilled in the 20th century

Jesus warned that Jerusalem would be dominated by Gentile nations until that period reached a determined end:

Luke 21:24

"And they will fall by the edge of the sword, and be led away captive into all nations. And Jerusalem will be trampled by Gentiles until the times of the Gentiles are fulfilled."

New King James Version (NKJV)

In 1948, the Jewish people — dispersed among the nations for nearly 1,900 years — saw their own nation reborn. Scripture had already anticipated something this unusual: a nation being born, in effect, "in a day":

Isaiah 66:8

"Who has heard such a thing? Who has seen such things? Shall the earth be made to give birth in one day? Or shall a nation be born at once?"

New King James Version (NKJV)

Then, in 1967, during the Six-Day War, Israel recaptured specifically the city of Jerusalem, which had been under Jordanian rule. This is what fulfills, in a more literal sense, the promise of Luke 21:24 concerning Jerusalem itself — not just the nation in general.

It is important not to treat these two dates as if they compete with one another: each fulfills a specific and distinct biblical promise. 1948 answers to the birth of the nation; 1967 answers to the end of Gentile dominion over the city of Jerusalem in particular. Both deserve equal weight when considering the evidence.

How far back does "the times of the Gentiles" really go? Foreign rule over Jerusalem did not begin with Rome — it had already been underway for nearly 600 years when Jesus spoke.
Read more

A clock that started long before Jesus

When Jesus speaks of Jerusalem being "trampled by Gentiles," He is not announcing the start of something new — He is describing the continuation of a state of affairs that had already been underway for nearly six centuries. Gentile dominion over Jerusalem began with the Babylonian conquest in 586 B.C., when Nebuchadnezzar destroyed Solomon's temple and carried the people away captive:

Daniel 2:37-38

"You, O king, are a king of kings. For the God of heaven has given you a kingdom, power, strength, and glory... and has made you ruler over them all."

New King James Version (NKJV)

The sequence of Gentile empires

From that point on, the same sequence of empires described in Daniel's visions (the statue of metals in Daniel 2, and the four beasts of Daniel 7) ruled successively over Jerusalem: Babylon → Medo-Persia → Greece → Rome. The only partial exception was the brief period of independence under the Hasmonean dynasty (roughly 140-63 B.C.), following the Maccabean revolt, until Rome took Jerusalem in 63 B.C.

After 70 A.D., Gentile rule continued through a long succession of powers: the Roman Empire, the Byzantine Empire, Arab caliphates, the Crusaders, the Mamluks, the Ottoman Empire, and finally the British Empire — until 1967.

Understanding this background does not change the conclusion that 1967 marks a significant end point, but it does give a fuller and more honest picture of when that clock really started: not with Jesus or with Rome, but nearly 600 years earlier, with Jerusalem's fall to Babylon.

A remnant that returns — twice

Isaiah also anticipated that God would gather His scattered people not once, but twice:

Isaiah 11:11

"It shall come to pass in that day that the Lord shall set His hand again the second time to recover the remnant of His people..."

New King James Version (NKJV)

The first gathering was the return of a remnant after the Babylonian exile. The second — of a people scattered for nearly two thousand years, not merely seventy — begins in the early twentieth century and culminates in 1948.

Topic 4

Answering Skepticism

"Where is the promise of His coming?" — Peter's warning about the scoffers of the last days

The apostle Peter anticipated that, over time, a mocking attitude toward the expectation of Christ's return would arise — even within the community of faith itself:

2 Peter 3:3-4

"Knowing this first: that scoffers will come in the last days, walking according to their own lusts, and saying, 'Where is the promise of His coming? For since the fathers fell asleep, all things continue as they were from the beginning of creation.'"

New King James Version (NKJV)

Peter responds by recalling that this same God has already intervened decisively in history: the worldwide flood, and the judgment on Sodom and Gomorrah. The fact that things "continue as they were" for a season does not mean God will not act.

2 Peter 3:8

"But, beloved, do not forget this one thing, that with the Lord one day is as a thousand years, and a thousand years as one day."

New King James Version (NKJV)
"Willfully forget." The rest of Peter's warning about past and future judgment.
Read more

Peter goes on to explain that these scoffers "willfully forget" that by the word of God the heavens and the earth were formed out of water and in the water, and that the world of that time perished, being flooded with water. That same God has reserved the present heavens and earth for fire, kept for the day of judgment.

Peter's central point is not to instill fear, but to correct memory: the apparent delay of Christ's return is not evidence that He will not come — it is an expression of God's patience, "not willing that any should perish, but that all should come to repentance" (2 Peter 3:9).

Topic 5

The Central Theme of Scripture

Why the return of Christ holds such a prominent place throughout the Bible

After salvation, the second coming of Christ is, according to this teaching, the most developed theme across the whole of Scripture. It is suggested that there are hundreds of prophecies fulfilled with precision concerning Christ's first coming, and a considerably larger number of prophetic references concerning His second coming — spread throughout both testaments.

This teaching does not seek to add a secondary theme to the Christian faith, but to recover the prominence that Scripture itself gives it.

Topic 6

Living in Expectation

The spiritual effect of actively expecting Christ's return, rather than mentally postponing it

The apostle John directly connects the hope of Christ's return with a process of personal purification:

1 John 3:2-3

"We know that when He is revealed, we shall be like Him, for we shall see Him as He is. And everyone who has this hope in Him purifies himself, just as He is pure."

New King James Version (NKJV)

Jesus Himself warned of the opposite effect — what happens when someone begins to think His return is far off.

Matthew 24:48-51

"But if that evil servant says in his heart, 'My master is delaying his coming,' and begins to beat his fellow servants, and to eat and drink with the drunkards, the master of that servant will come on a day when he is not looking for him..."

New King James Version (NKJV)

The logic is clear: mentally postponing the expectation of Christ's return makes it easier to give in to the desires of the flesh. Living with an active hope of His coming, by contrast, produces a real motivation for daily holiness.

Topic 7

Six Thousand Years and the Seventh Day

An ancient framework, held by both Jewish teachers and the early church fathers, concerning the ages of human history

The prophet Hosea describes Israel's future restoration using the language of "days," in a passage many interpreters connect to the ages of history:

Hosea 6:1-2

"Come, and let us return to the LORD... After two days He will revive us; on the third day He will raise us up, that we may live in His sight."

New King James Version (NKJV)

This language of "days" rests on a principle that also appears in the Psalms and in Peter:

2 Peter 3:8 (see also Psalm 90:4)

"With the Lord one day is as a thousand years, and a thousand years as one day."

New King James Version (NKJV)

With this key, "the third day" in Hosea is understood as the third thousand-year period after a reference point, corresponding to the millennial reign described in Revelation 20. This same pattern is echoed in Job:

Job 5:19

"He shall deliver you in six troubles, yes, in seven no evil shall touch you."

New King James Version (NKJV)

Both ancient Jewish teachers and the early church fathers taught a framework of six thousand years of human history, followed by a seventh thousand-year period of messianic reign — paralleling the six days of creation and the seventh day of rest.

Testimonies from the early centuries

Several Christian writers from the first three centuries — some discipled by those who knew the apostles directly — expressed this same expectation of a six-thousand-year framework:

  • Epistle of Barnabas, 15:7-9: "in six days, that is, in six thousand years, all things will be finished."
  • Irenaeus, Against Heresies, 5:28: "a day of the Lord is as a thousand years... and in six days created things were completed; it is evident, therefore, that they will come to an end at the six thousandth year."
  • Hippolytus, Commentary on Daniel, 2:4 (c. A.D. 205): "the Sabbath is a type of the coming kingdom... it follows that six thousand years must be fulfilled."
  • Commodianus (c. A.D. 240): "we will be immortal when the six thousand years are completed."

Four ages of two thousand years each

This framework is traditionally organized into four ages: the age of chaos/creation (from Adam to Abraham), the age of law/Torah (from Abraham to Jesus), the age of grace/the church (our current era), and finally the age of the kingdom (the millennium).

A calculation based on biblical genealogies

Using the genealogies of Genesis, Chronicles, and Ezra, the following approximate chronology is proposed:

  • Adam, created around 3925 B.C.
  • +2,000 years → the time of Abraham, around 1925 B.C.
  • +2,000 years → destruction of the Second Temple, A.D. 70-75
  • +2,000 years (the age of grace) → approximately the year A.D. 2075

This genealogical calculation is independent of the "generation" calculations presented in Topic 12, and is best treated as a separate line of evidence, with its own level of certainty (and of speculation).

Topic 8

Church, Israel, and the Saints of the Tribulation

Three distinct groups in the prophetic plan that are worth keeping separate

This teaching distinguishes three groups that are sometimes blended together when discussing the last days: the Church (the body of Christ formed since Pentecost), Israel (the Jewish people, with their own covenant and their own prophetic future), and the saints of the tribulation (those who come to faith during the seven-year period described in Revelation, after the Church's rapture).

One argument offered in favor of this distinction is that the Church, explicitly mentioned in Revelation 2-3, is not named again as such until Revelation 21 — which is read as a sign that the Church is not present during the chapters describing the tribulation.

The prophet Daniel had received, centuries earlier, a specific time frame for the Jewish people: seventy "weeks" of years, sixty-nine of which were already fulfilled with the coming of the Messiah — leaving still one final week, of seven years, to be fulfilled. This final week has remained pending because, after Israel's rejection of the Messiah, a partial and temporary hardening came upon the Jewish people (Romans 11:25), while God grafts in the Gentiles — described as a "wild olive tree" — into the root of the promise (Romans 11:17). When "the fullness of the Gentiles has come in" (Romans 11:25), God will once again deal specially with Israel, completing the seventieth week that was left in pause.

Daniel 9:24-27 (summary)

Topic 9

Apostasy and Revival

Two movements that, according to this teaching, are happening at the same time in the current generation

Paul warned that before the full revelation of the "man of sin," a great apostasy — a widespread falling away from the faith — had to come first:

2 Thessalonians 2:3

"Let no one deceive you by any means; for that Day will not come unless the falling away comes first..."

New King James Version (NKJV)

Various recent studies have pointed to a notable departure from organized faith in several Western countries in recent decades — a trend which, according to this teaching, would fulfill that warning.

At the same time, it is held that a revival of unusual proportions is coming, compared even to the outpouring described in the book of Acts:

Haggai 2:9

"'The glory of this latter temple shall be greater than the former,' says the LORD of hosts..."

New King James Version (NKJV)

James describes this pattern using the agricultural image of Israel's rains:

James 5:7

"Therefore be patient, brethren, until the coming of the Lord. See how the farmer waits for the precious fruit of the earth, waiting patiently for it until it receives the early and latter rain."

New King James Version (NKJV)

Just as there was an "early rain" at Pentecost, a "latter rain" is expected — a final outpouring before the full harvest.

Topic 10

Reasons for a Pre-Tribulation Rapture

Two central passages that support this teaching's pre-tribulational view

Isaiah describes a sequence in which the dead are raised and God's people are sheltered just before His indignation is poured out on the earth:

Isaiah 26:19-21

"Your dead shall live... Come, my people, enter your chambers, and shut your doors behind you; hide yourself, as it were, for a little moment, until the indignation is past. For behold, the LORD comes out of His place to punish the inhabitants of the earth for their iniquity..."

New King James Version (NKJV)

Paul describes this same event as a "mystery" — something not clearly revealed before his own apostolic revelation of it:

1 Thessalonians 4:16-17

"For the Lord Himself will descend from heaven with a shout, with the voice of an archangel, and with the trumpet of God. And the dead in Christ will rise first. Then we who are alive and remain shall be caught up together with them in the clouds to meet the Lord in the air. And thus we shall always be with the Lord."

New King James Version (NKJV)

It is argued that it would be incoherent for these resurrected dead to have to remain hidden on earth throughout the seven years of tribulation; this rapture is therefore understood as an event that occurs before that period of judgment begins.

It is worth noting, honestly, that this is one of the points where there is significant disagreement among faithful, serious students of Scripture — as explained in the next topic.

Topic 11

Before, in the Middle, or After?

Three views within the Christian faith on the timing of the rapture

On the fact that Christ is coming, there is full agreement among all Christians who hold any of these views. The difference lies solely in the order: does the Church's rapture happen before, in the middle of, or after the seven-year tribulation period described in Revelation?

We present the three main views here in a balanced way. No Christian should divide from another over holding one or the other.

Pre-Tribulationism

Holds that the Church is raptured before the seven-year tribulation begins. Among the arguments offered: that the Church is not mentioned between Revelation 4 and 19 (while the tribulation is described there); that 1 Thessalonians 5:9 says believers "are not appointed to wrath, but to obtain salvation"; and that the rapture is presented as an imminent, comforting hope (1 Thessalonians 4:18), not as something requiring judgment to be endured first.

Post-Tribulationism

Holds that the Church remains on earth throughout the tribulation and is raptured at the end, just before or together with Christ's visible return. It draws support, among other passages, from Matthew 24:29-31, where the Son of Man gathers "His elect" after the tribulation described in those verses, and from the argument that the early church, which suffered real persecution, did not expect to be exempted from suffering.

Mid-Tribulationism (and intermediate variants)

Intermediate views that place the rapture at some point within the seven years — commonly around the midpoint, when the covenant is broken according to Daniel 9:27 — holding that the Church endures the human persecution of the first half, but is removed before God's wrath is specifically poured out in the second half.

What if there is no literal tribulation, no literal millennium, and no rapture as a separate event? Other historic frameworks within the Christian faith.
Read more

The three views above (pre-trib, mid-trib, post-trib) all share the same underlying framework, called premillennialism: all of them assume a literal future seven-year tribulation and a literal future millennial reign of Christ on earth. But within the history of the Church, other complete traditions do not start from that same framework.

Amillennialism

Does not expect a literal, future thousand-year reign. It understands the "thousand years" of Revelation 20 as describing, symbolically, Christ's present reign from heaven together with the saints who have already departed, throughout the whole period between His first and second coming. In this framework there is, strictly speaking, no "rapture" followed by a literal seven-year tribulation: Christ's second coming, the general resurrection, and the final judgment occur as a single event. This has been a widely held position throughout the historic Christian tradition, including much of Reformed theology.

Postmillennialism

Holds that Christ's kingdom is established progressively in history, through the preaching of the gospel and the gradual transformation of the nations — a reading supported by parables such as the mustard seed or the leaven (Matthew 13:31-33), and by passages such as Isaiah 11:9 ("the earth shall be full of the knowledge of the LORD, as the waters cover the sea"). According to this view, Christ returns after that long period of the kingdom's advance, not before. It was an influential position at certain points in church history, particularly between the 17th and 19th centuries.

Preterism (partial or full)

Understands that most of the prophecies in Matthew 24 and Revelation were already fulfilled in the first century, chiefly with the destruction of Jerusalem in A.D. 70. Partial preterism holds this for most of the signs, but still regards the final resurrection, judgment, and Christ's bodily second coming as future — this is a position held by serious evangelical Christians. Full preterism, on the other hand, holds that even the second coming itself already occurred spiritually in A.D. 70; this version is considered, by the great majority of Christian traditions, to fall outside the bounds of historic orthodoxy, precisely because it denies a future, bodily coming of Christ.

This teaching is developed within the premillennial framework — and, within it, holds the pre-tribulational view — but it is only fair to acknowledge that this is not the only reading held by faithful Christians throughout church history.

This teaching holds the pre-tribulational view, with the biblical reasons presented in Topic 10. The other views are included here so it is clear that this is a matter of interpretation among faithful believers, not a closed point of essential doctrine.
Topic 12

In What Period Does Christ Return?

Three independent lines of biblical calculation, and why none of them proposes an exact date
Important clarification: none of these calculations claims to pinpoint an exact year for Christ's return. Jesus Himself said that "no one knows the day or hour" (Matthew 24:36). What these lines of evidence aim to show is a season — a broad range within which this fulfillment would fall — not a specific date that could be marked on a calendar.

With that said, there are three distinct ways of approaching the calculation, and each is worth examining separately, because they do not all carry the same biblical weight.

Line 1 — Two thousand years since Christ's resurrection

Based on the idea that "the age of grace" lasts 2,000 years (a symbolic reading of 2 Peter 3:8 and Psalm 90:4, "a thousand years in Your sight are like yesterday"):

Starting pointCalculationResult
Christ's death and resurrection (~A.D. 32)32 + 2,000A.D. 2032

This calculation does not depend on the concept of "generation" — it is simply adding 2,000 symbolic years to a historical event.

Line 2 — A "generation" since Israel's return to its land

Two independent decisions come into play here, and it is important not to conflate them.

(a) What is the starting point? — 1948 (the nation's birth) or 1967 (the recapture of Jerusalem, see Topic 3).

(b) How long does a "generation" last? — this is where more care is needed:

  • 70-80 years — according to Psalm 90:10: "The days of our lives are seventy years; and if by reason of strength they are eighty years..." This is the only figure Scripture itself gives for the length of a human generation.
  • 100 years — a figure that does not come from Scripture, but from a modern dictionary definition describing "generation" as "people living within the same period of time," illustrated with the example that a newborn can be alive at the same time as someone nearly 100 years old. This figure conflates who counts as a contemporary with how long a generation lasts as a unit of time, and therefore rests on considerably weaker biblical support than Psalm 90.

Combining these two decisions, without discarding either starting date:

Starting pointLength of "generation"Result
1948 (birth of the nation)70-80 years (Psalm 90:10)2018 – 2028
1967 (recapture of Jerusalem)70-80 years (Psalm 90:10)2037 – 2047
1967 (recapture of Jerusalem)100 years (dictionary)2067 (weaker basis)

Line 3 — Genealogical calculation of the two-thousand-year ages

As detailed in the note under Topic 7, the calculation based on biblical genealogies and the early church fathers' teaching on four ages of 2,000 years each arrives at approximately the year A.D. 2075.

What this shows, taken together

It is not that all three lines point to the exact same year — they do not. They point, to varying degrees, toward a shared, broad window, roughly between 2018 and 2075, with several possible combinations within that range. The line with the most direct biblical support for the length of a "generation" (Psalm 90:10) actually gives the earliest and least forced result of the group.

The value of these lines is not mathematical precision, but something simpler: recognizing that, on this reading, we are living within the season — and that is exactly what Scripture calls us to do with this information: not to calculate a day, but to live prepared.

Topic 13

Be Ready, Not Focused on Dates

The true purpose of this teaching: personal readiness, not calculation

Revelation describes the Church, at the moment of the marriage supper of the Lamb, as a bride who has made herself ready:

Revelation 19:7-8

"...the marriage of the Lamb has come, and His wife has made herself ready. And to her it was granted to be arrayed in fine linen, clean and bright, for the fine linen is the righteous acts of the saints."

New King James Version (NKJV)

Notice that it is she who makes herself ready. John describes two possible reactions to Christ's appearing: confidence, or shame.

1 John 2:28

"And now, little children, abide in Him, that when He appears, we may have confidence and not be ashamed before Him at His coming."

New King James Version (NKJV)

The central promise of this whole message is summarized in Christ's own words at the close of Scripture:

Revelation 22:12, 20

"And behold, I am coming quickly, and My reward is with Me, to give to every one according to his work... Surely I am coming quickly.' Amen. Even so, come, Lord Jesus!"

New King James Version (NKJV)

Paul himself clarifies the purpose of all prophetic teaching: not to sow fear, but mutual comfort.

1 Thessalonians 4:18

"Therefore comfort one another with these words."

New King James Version (NKJV)
Topic 14

The Day of Your Encounter With Christ

Why readiness is necessary no matter which of these two scenarios you experience

For each person, the personal encounter with Christ comes in one of two ways: either the Lord calls us home through death, or Christ returns while we are still alive. Christians have lived with this tension for 2,000 years — entire generations died waiting for Christ's visible return, and for them, the encounter with Him came through death, not through His coming in the clouds.

2 Corinthians 5:8

"We are confident, yes, well pleased rather to be absent from the body and to be present with the Lord."

New King James Version (NKJV)
Hebrews 9:27

"And as it is appointed for men to die once, but after this the judgment..."

New King James Version (NKJV)

This does not diminish the hope of Christ's return — quite the opposite: it shows that readiness is necessary in either scenario, not only if we happen to witness His coming while alive. That is why the question that matters most is not "when exactly will this happen?" but "am I ready today?" — regardless of whether your view is pre-tribulational, mid-tribulational, post-tribulational, or whether you simply hold no settled position on the matter.

"Behold, I am coming quickly."
Revelation 22:12
